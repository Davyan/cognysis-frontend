⏱️ Pause Patterns

What it measures: How long the person stops between words, phrases, and sentences. Both silent pauses and filled pauses ("um," "uh") are counted.
Why it matters: Longer pauses often mean the brain is struggling to retrieve the next word from memory. In early dementia, this "word-finding pause" is one of the most reliable early signs.
Average Pause Duration
1.4 seconds (normal: 0.6s)
2.3× longer than expected. Each extra second of silence represents cognitive effort to retrieve words.
Pause Frequency
0.42 pauses per word (normal: 0.18)
Pauses happen more than twice as often as in healthy speech. Suggests frequent word-retrieval failures.
Within-Word Pauses
3 instances (normal: 0)
Stopping mid-word is rare in healthy speech. Indicates severe retrieval breakdown.
Filled Pauses ("um," "uh")
4 instances in 4 minutes (normal: 1)
Using filler words to buy thinking time. Common in early cognitive decline.

🗣️ Voice Quality

What it measures: Stability and clarity of the voice — tremors, hoarseness, breathiness, and how "clean" the vocal signal is.
Why it matters: The vocal cords are controlled by tiny muscles. As cognitive function declines, the brain's control over these muscles can weaken, leading to subtle voice changes invisible to the human ear but detectable by AI.
Jitter (Pitch Instability)
1.82% (normal: <1.0%)
Voice pitch wobbles slightly from cycle to cycle. Higher jitter = less stable vocal cord control.
Shimmer (Loudness Instability)
6.4% (normal: <4.0%)
Voice volume fluctuates unexpectedly. Suggests inconsistent breath support and muscle control.
Harmonics-to-Noise Ratio (HNR)
12.1 dB (normal: >20 dB)
How "clean" the voice is vs. how "noisy." Lower HNR = more breathiness and less clear tone.
Voice Breaks
2 instances (normal: 0)
Moments where the voice cuts out briefly. Indicates intermittent loss of vocal cord closure.

⚡ Speech Rate

What it measures: How fast the person speaks, how smoothly words flow together, and the rhythm of syllables.
Why it matters: Slower speech often reflects the brain needing more time to plan and execute each word. In dementia, articulation can become more effortful as motor planning declines.
Articulation Rate
3.2 words/sec (normal: 4.1)
22% slower than expected. Each word takes more effort to produce clearly.
Syllabic Interval Variability
0.18 (normal: 0.12)
Timing between syllables is irregular. Healthy speech has a steady, predictable rhythm.
Phonemes Per Second
8.4 (normal: 11.2)
Fewer individual sounds produced per second. Suggests simplified or slowed articulation.

🎵 Pitch & Prosody

What it measures: How much the voice rises and falls in tone — the "melody" of speech.
Why it matters: A monotone voice can indicate reduced emotional expression and declining motor control of the larynx. In Alzheimer's, prosody (speech melody) often flattens early.
Pitch Variability (F0 Range)
↓ 34% below age norm
Voice stays in a narrow pitch range. Less expressive variation in tone.
Intonation Contour
Flat (normal: varied)
Questions don't rise, statements don't fall. Speech sounds more robotic.
Monotony Index
0.72 (normal: <0.40)
High score = very little pitch movement. Strong indicator of declining vocal control.

📊 Spectral Features

What it measures: Fine-grained sound patterns that reveal the physical shape and movement of the vocal tract — vocal cords, tongue, lips.
Why it matters: These features capture subtle changes in how speech is physically produced. They can detect neurological changes before they become obvious to human listeners.
MFCC Variance
0.89 (normal: 1.2)
Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients — the "fingerprint" of voice timbre. Lower variance = less dynamic vocal tract movement.
Formant Dispersion (F1–F4)
↓ 18% below norm
Formants are resonance peaks that shape vowel sounds. Reduced dispersion suggests less precise tongue positioning.
Spectral Slope
-8.2 (normal: -6.5)
How energy is distributed across high vs. low frequencies. Steeper slope = less high-frequency energy = less crisp consonants.

⏳ Response Timing

What it measures: How long it takes to answer a question, how long each speaking turn lasts, and how actively the person engages in back-and-forth conversation.
Why it matters: Delayed responses can indicate slower information processing. Shorter, less interactive turns may reflect reduced engagement or difficulty following the conversation flow.
Response Latency
2.8 seconds (normal: 1.5s)
Takes almost twice as long to start answering. Suggests slower processing speed.
Average Turn Duration
18.4 seconds (normal: 28s)
Shorter speaking turns. Person gives brief answers rather than elaborating — possible reduced confidence in memory.
Dialog Interactivity
0.41 (normal: 0.65)
Lower score = less back-and-forth. Person responds but doesn't actively build on the conversation.

📚 Vocabulary Diversity

What it measures: How many different words the person uses compared to how many total words they speak. More variety = richer vocabulary.
Why it matters: As memory declines, people tend to rely on a smaller set of familiar words. They may say "thing" or "stuff" instead of specific names. This narrowing of vocabulary is one of the earliest and most reliable signs.
Type-Token Ratio (TTR)
0.31 (normal: >0.50)
Only 31% of words were unique. In healthy speech, over half are unique. Strong signal of vocabulary shrinkage.
Moving Average TTR
0.28 (normal: 0.48)
Even when measured in short windows, vocabulary stays repetitive. Confirms it's not just one topic being discussed.
Unique Word Count
47 words (normal: 85+)
Far fewer different words used in a 4-minute sample. Suggests reduced access to semantic memory.
Brunet's Index
12.4 (normal: 8.0)
Higher score = less lexical richness. Mathematical confirmation of vocabulary decline.

🔗 Semantic Coherence

What it measures: Whether ideas connect logically and stay on topic. Does the conversation make sense from sentence to sentence?
Why it matters: In dementia, people may start talking about one thing and drift to another without realizing it. Or they may give vague, empty responses that don't really answer the question. This "semantic emptiness" is a hallmark of memory-related decline.
Topic Maintenance Score
0.48 (threshold: 0.65)
Conversation drifts off-topic. When asked about vegetables, she described them as "the green ones" and "the long ones" — vague and non-specific.
Word Embedding Coherence
0.48 (threshold: 0.65)
AI measures how closely related the words are in meaning. Low score = words don't form a coherent semantic cluster.
Information Content
↓ 22% below norm
Each sentence carries less factual detail. "I went to the market" vs. "I went to Coronation Market to buy callaloo and carrots."
Causal Link Density
0.12 (normal: 0.28)
Fewer "because," "so," "then" connections. Narratives become a list of events rather than a connected story.

🔍 Word-Finding Ability

What it measures: How often the person struggles to name things, uses vague substitutes, or talks around a word they can't recall.
Why it matters: Word-finding difficulty is often the very first symptom patients and families notice. The person knows what they want to say but can't retrieve the exact word. They may say "the thing you cook with" instead of "stove."
Circumlocutions
3 instances (normal: 0)
Talking around a word: "the green ones" instead of "callaloo," "the long ones" instead of "cucumber."
Vague Word Usage
6 instances (normal: 1)
Using generic words: "thing," "stuff," "something," "that place." Avoids specific names.
Naming Errors
2 instances (normal: 0)
Calling something by the wrong name, or giving up and changing the subject.
Semantic Clustering
0.31 (normal: 0.60)
When listing items (e.g., vegetables), words don't come from the same semantic category. Suggests disorganized semantic memory.

🏗️ Sentence Structure

What it measures: Complexity and completeness of sentences — grammar, length, and whether thoughts are fully expressed.
Why it matters: As executive function declines, people may use shorter, simpler sentences. They may drop words, leave sentences unfinished, or lose track of grammatical structure. In FTD (language variant), this can be especially pronounced.
Syntactic Complexity
↓ 28% below norm
Sentences are shorter and simpler. Fewer subordinate clauses ("because," "although," "while").
Sentence Fragments
4 instances (normal: 0)
Sentences that start but don't finish: "I went to the..." trailing off. Indicates loss of grammatical planning.
Grammatical Errors
2 instances (normal: 0)
Mismatched subjects and verbs, dropped articles ("the," "a"), or incorrect word order.
Mean Sentence Length
6.2 words (normal: 9.5)
Shorter sentences may reflect difficulty holding complex thoughts in working memory.

💬 Disfluency Markers

What it measures: "Um," "uh," word repetitions, restarts, and self-corrections. Everyone does these, but frequency matters.
Why it matters: Some disfluency is normal. But excessive "ums" and repetitions can signal the brain is buffering — buying time while searching for the right word. The system distinguishes normal disfluency from dementia-related patterns.
Filled Pause Rate
8.4% (normal: 2.5%)
"Um" and "uh" appear more than 3× as often as normal. Suggests frequent word-retrieval delays.
Word Repetitions
5 instances (normal: 1)
Repeating a word immediately: "I went to to the market." Indicates motor planning glitch.
Self-Corrections
2 instances (normal: 3)
Surprisingly few self-corrections. Healthy speakers often catch and fix their own errors. Fewer corrections may mean reduced self-monitoring.
Restart Rate
3 instances (normal: 0)
Abandoning a sentence and starting over: "I went to... yesterday I went to the market." Indicates lost train of thought.

🧠 Memory Language

What it measures: Phrases that reveal uncertainty about memory — "I think," "maybe," "probably," "I don't remember exactly."
Why it matters: People with early memory decline often develop "verbal hedging" — they qualify statements because they're not sure. "I think my grandson is 12" instead of "My grandson is 12." This metacognitive awareness (knowing you might be wrong) is actually an early sign.
Uncertainty Markers
4.2% of words (normal: 1.5%)
"I think," "maybe," "probably," "sort of" appear 3× more than normal. Suggests reduced confidence in memory accuracy.
Temporal Confusion
2 instances (normal: 0)
Mixing up when things happened: "Last week... or was it the week before?" Difficulty sequencing events.
Episodic Detail
↓ 35% below norm
When recalling events, provides fewer specific details (who, what, where, when). "We went out" vs. "We went to Devon House for ice cream on Saturday."
Confidence Declarations
0 instances (normal: 2)
Rarely says "I know" or "I remember clearly." Absence of confident memory statements is notable.

Clinical Recommendations

📋 In-Person Cognitive Tests
MoCA (education-adjusted), memory word lists, verbal fluency (name as many animals as you can in 1 minute), clock drawing, trail-making tests. These confirm whether the voice-based findings match standardized paper tests.
🩺 Brain Scan (MRI)
Checks for shrinkage in the hippocampus (memory center) — the hallmark of Alzheimer's. Also rules out strokes, tumors, or fluid buildup that can mimic dementia.
💊 Review Diabetes & Blood Pressure
Uncontrolled diabetes and high blood pressure can damage brain blood vessels and worsen memory. Optimizing these can slow decline or even improve cognition.
👨‍👩‍👧 Caregiver Support
Connect family to Jamaica Alzheimer's Association. Provide education on what to expect. Assess caregiver stress — burnout is common and preventable with early support.
